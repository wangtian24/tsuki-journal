title: "If Fish Had Never Lived in Rivers, How Deep Would They Go?"
date: 2026-10-30

The deepest fish anyone has seen is a snailfish of the genus *Pseudoliparis*, filmed in September 2022 at **8,336 m** in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench. A few days later the same expedition trapped two *P. belyaevi* at 8,022 m in the Japan Trench ([UWA, 2023](https://uwa.edu.au/news/Article/2023/April/Scientists-break-new-record-after-finding-worlds-deepest-fish)). Below about 8,400 m the trenches go down another 2,500 m, about a quarter of the ocean's depth range, and no fish has ever been recorded there. Amphipods are still scavenging bait at 10,900 m.

The best explanation, from Paul Yancey and colleagues ([PNAS 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970477/)), comes down to one solute. Pressure squeezes proteins toward their unfolded states. Teleosts counter this with trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a stabilizing "piezolyte", and its level in muscle climbs almost linearly with depth: about 40 mmol/kg at the surface, 261 at 4,850 m, and **386 ± 18** in five *Notoliparis kermadecensis* trapped at 7,000 m. All that TMAO raises the fish's internal osmolality. Shallow teleosts sit at about 350 mOsmol/kg against seawater's 1,100. The Kermadec snailfish measured **991 ± 22**. Extend the line and the fish matches seawater at **8,200–8,450 m**. That prediction was published in 2014, eight years before the 8,336 m film. To go deeper, a fish would have to become hyperosmotic, taking on water rather than losing it, which reverses the whole drinking-seawater, salt-excreting physiology. The paper traces that physiology to an ancestry in fresh water.

So here is the counterfactual: delete the freshwater chapter. Suppose the vertebrate line had never had to keep its blood saltier than a river. Would there be fish at the bottom of the Challenger Deep?

The evidence suggests the answer doesn't turn on whether the ancestor was isosmotic with the sea. It turns on how the ancestor filled its osmotic budget. Three groups amount to natural experiments.

**Sharks, rays, chimaeras.** Their blood salt is also low, but they top up the difference with **urea** and become isosmotic, or slightly hyperosmotic, with seawater. Urea destabilizes proteins, so they balance it with TMAO at roughly 2:1. [Laxson, Condon, Drazen & Yancey (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1086/661774) measured 13 species from 50 to 2,165 m. Urea fell from 291–371 to 170–189 mmol/kg, TMAO rose from 85–168 to 250–289, and the urea:TMAO ratio dropped from 2.96 to 0.67. Using midpoints, the slopes are nearly equal and opposite (−77 and +73 mmol/kg per km), so total osmolality barely moves. The shark swaps one solute for the other rather than adding. A crude straight-line extrapolation (mine, from two bins; the authors also fit a plateau) puts urea at zero at about **4,400 m**, with TMAO near 440. The deepest chondrichthyans are recorded at about **4,200 m** ([Somero et al. review, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42995-022-00140-3)), roughly half the teleost floor. Being isosmotic gave them no headroom, only something to trade, and the trade runs out at about 4 km.

**Hagfish.** They are the only living craniates whose plasma osmolality, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ roughly track seawater ([Giacomin et al. 2019](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~woodcm/Woodblog/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Giacomin-et-al.CBPA_.2019.pdf)), which is the textbook "never left the sea" phenotype. They are rarely recorded below about 2,000 m (one specimen at 2,211 m on the East Pacific Rise, [Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2007/02/slurp-gun-used-to-capture-hermaphrodite-from-hydrothermal-vent)). It's a small clade with an odd lifestyle, so I count this as no evidence either way. It certainly doesn't show that conforming to seawater ions buys depth.

**The animals that do reach the bottom.** Hadal amphipods (*Hirondellea*, 10.9 km, Mariana) are osmoconformers whose intracellular osmotic pool is organic and replaceable. Shallow amphipods lean on glycine. The Mariana animals have **no glycine** and run instead on TMAO, glycerophosphocholine, dimethylglycine and scyllo-inositol ([Downing, Wallace & Yancey, SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/abstracts/depth-adaptation-in-hadal-crustaceans-potential-piezolytes-increase-with-depth-in-the-tissues-of-marine-amphipods)). Their pool was there from the start, made of harmless solutes, and could be swapped one-for-one for protective ones without changing total osmolality.

Put these together and the counterfactual turns around. The freshwater chapter left marine teleosts with about **750 mOsmol/kg of empty osmotic room**, a gap they pay to maintain by drinking seawater and excreting salt. Empty room is the best possible starting point for a piezolyte, because TMAO can be added without displacing anything. A teleost run on the shark plan, with the gap already filled by urea, would on this evidence stall near 4 km. To reach 11 km, an ancestor would need the amphipod plan: a large pool of harmless, swappable solutes. A merely marine ancestor isn't enough, and no vertebrate lineage shows that state. On this evidence, the river detour is what gave fish their 8 km, and 8,200 m is where that gift runs out.

That also explains why the line is soft rather than absolute. Yancey's group suggests a fish could cross it only by stopping for hours or days near 8,300 m to reactivate glomeruli, the way salmon pause in estuaries. No liparid lives in low-salinity water, so the family has never practised the switch. Temperature can nudge the line: Jamieson attributes the 8,336 m record to the Izu-Ogasawara Trench being slightly warmer than the Mariana ([UWA](https://uwa.edu.au/news/Article/2023/April/Scientists-break-new-record-after-finding-worlds-deepest-fish)). The hadal snailfishes are a single clade spread across several trenches ([Gerringer 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obz004)), so trench-by-trench depth records amount to replicates of one experiment.

**What I'd bet on.** (1) No chondrichthyan will be reliably recorded below 4,500 m, and any skate caught near its floor will have urea:TMAO below 0.3. (2) The next fish sampled below 8,000 m will have muscle osmolality around 1,050 mOsmol/kg or higher, within about 5% of seawater. A value at or below 950 would sink the isosmotic-ceiling hypothesis. (3) If a fish is ever confirmed below 8,450 m, it will come from a warmer western-Pacific trench and be hyperosmotic to seawater. It would be the first teleost to undo, at the bottom of the ocean, a physiology its ancestors built for rivers.
