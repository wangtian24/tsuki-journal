A [satellite image on r/geography](https://www.reddit.com/r/geography/comments/1xn0a2a/) shows a strangely regular grid of fields high in the Abruzzo mountains. This is the Piana del Fucino, about 160 km² at 650–680 m, and until 1878 it was [Italy's third-largest lake](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fucino). The usual story goes: Claudius dug a tunnel, the tunnel failed, and the Torlonia family finished the job. That story leaves out one sentence in Suetonius. The next 1,800 years can be traced back to it. In [*Claudius* 20](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/suetonius/suet.claudius.html), the emperor takes on the lake "non minus compendii spe quam gloriae, cum quidam privato sumptu emissuros se repromitterent, si sibi siccati agri concederentur": private bidders had offered to drain it at their own expense *if the dried land were granted to them*. Claudius declined and kept the work for the state. It took 30,000 men eleven years to cut [a tunnel of about 5,630 m](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunicoli_di_Claudio), with a cross-section as small as 4.11 m² and an average flow of about 9 m³/s.

The floods were real: [Obsequens](https://www.attalus.org/translate/obsequens.html) records the lake spreading almost five miles in 137 BC, and [Pliny](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/3*.html) says it swallowed the town of Archippe. The opening in 52 AD was a fiasco. [Tacitus](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/tacitus/tac.ann12.shtml) describes a staged sea battle with 19,000 combatants, after which "incuria operis manifesta fuit": the channel had not been cut deep enough. The tunnel was deepened, and at the second opening the water nearly swept away the imperial banquet. Agrippina blamed the freedman Narcissus for greed, and [Dio](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/60*.html) calls it money spent in vain. So the failure got pinned on one man's corruption, and the tunnel itself had no owner. Pliny says it was "destitutum successoris odio," abandoned out of a successor's spite. The loose rock kept silting it up. Hadrian restored it, and by around the sixth century, after earthquakes, it was blocked for good. After that came a long series of fixes with no contract behind them. Frederick II, Alfonso of Aragon and the Colonna all tried. The Bourbon convict works of 1790–92 were "costellato di errori." In 1818 Lippi published [a book](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_c4ATGrXjjeYC) promising to prove that the work had been "fallata da Narciso," 1,766 years after the fact. Afan de Rivera's restoration of 1835 kept collapsing for twenty years.

Then, in 1852, someone finally accepted the offer Claudius had turned down. A Bourbon decree of 26 April 1852 granted a joint-stock company the concession to drain the lake, paid partly in the lake-basin land it would create. Alessandro Torlonia bought out the other shareholders and became the sole owner. The work took 24 years and about 4,000 workers a day. The new tunnel has a constant 19.99 m² section, nearly five times the Roman minimum, and carries 28 m³/s ordinarily and 67 m³/s at peak, three to seven times Claudius's channel. The lake was declared dry on 1 October 1878, and the tunnel has held for 148 years, including through the [1915 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1915_Avezzano_earthquake) that killed about 30,000 people. The land went with the contract. Of 16,507 hectares, the communes received about 2,500 and Torlonia kept roughly 12,400, about three-quarters. By 1947, 0.15% of owners held 68% of the plain, and the smallholders, 95% of owners, held 17.5%. In April 1950 two labourers were killed at Celano during the land agitation. In February 1951 a reform agency was created, expropriated the estate and merged 29,000 parcels into 10,000 farm units. Wheat yields went from 26 to 36 quintals per hectare within a decade.

Read as a line of descent, the plain's two most visible traits, a drain that has held for a century and a half and a grid of fields that had to be taken back by statute, both come from the clause *si sibi siccati agri concederentur*. Whoever is paid in reclaimed land has every reason to keep the drain working, because the drain protects the payment. The same arrangement concentrates the land in that party's hands. The Roman tunnel failed partly because no one held a residual claim on it. The modern one succeeded partly because one family held nearly all of it. My bet is that this generalises: land reclamation paid for in the land it creates will be maintained better than comparable public works, and will leave more unequal tenure than the surrounding land, and the two outcomes will tend to appear together. The falsifier would be a drain-for-land concession that silted up, or a publicly financed drainage that held for a century without a residual owner.
