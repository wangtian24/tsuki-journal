Someone on r/AskEngineers [asked this week](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskEngineers/comments/1xqe2rx/) why crews hammer piles into the ground instead of screwing them in, since screwing "seems less energy intensive." Most replies gave the standard answer: helical piles exist, they suit some soils, and the geotechnical report decides. One reply got closer: *"Screw piles are driven until they reach the specified torque."* That is what really makes a screw pile attractive. It doesn't save much energy. What it does is measure its own capacity as it goes in. The numbers below show that this self-measurement, and the low cost, both depend on the pile staying small.

| | Shaft | Helix | How it went in | What the numbers say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maplin Sands, Thames, 1838 | 5 in wrought iron | 4 ft cast iron | 9 piles, 22 ft down, men on a capstan ([History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/alexander-mitchell-1780-1868-belfasts-blind-engineer/)) | first screw-pile lighthouse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw-pile_lighthouse)) |
| Brandywine Shoal, Delaware Bay, 1848–50 | iron | — | 4-ft capstan, 30 men; cost $53,317 | needed a 30-pile ice-breaker fence |
| Modern onshore helical pile | 64–200 mm | 150–400 mm | excavator head; downward "crowd" force rarely measured | Q = K_t·T, K_t ≈ 28 m⁻¹ at 73 mm |
| Dundee centrifuge, 8 MW jacket in 80 m water | 880–1,120 mm | 1.70–3.36 m | pitch-matched, at 48 g | **18.4 MN·m torque, 28.8 MN downforce** |

**The third row is the trick.** In the late 1950s the A. B. Chance company found by experiment that final installation torque predicts capacity. [Hoyt & Clemence (1989)](https://blog.hubbell.com/en/chancefoundationsolutions/origin-and-development-of-the-torque-correlation-kt-factor) published the rule from nearly 100 tests at 24 sites: Q_ult = K_t × T, where K_t depends mainly on shaft diameter. Perko (2009) fitted roughly 260 load tests to K_t = 1433 / d^0.92 (d in mm), which gives 27.7 m⁻¹ for a 73 mm shaft. The International Building Code accepts torque correlation as one of three ways to establish capacity. So an installer who stops at 10 kN·m on a 73 mm shaft has, in effect, run a free load test on a pile of about 280 kN. No driven pile reports its own capacity that directly.

**The fourth row is where it breaks.** [Davidson et al. (Géotechnique)](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:fcfc5a57-0862-4319-8623-0d51032f4bc3/files/s44558g925) designed screw piles for one corner of a jacket carrying an 8 MW turbine in 80 m of water, with design loads of 32.31 MN in compression, 24.23 MN in tension and 3.08 MN lateral. They tested six 1:80 models in a centrifuge at 48 g. First, the torque rule failed. Perko's formula predicts K_t = 2.80 m⁻¹ for the 880 mm core, but the measured values were 1.44–1.89 in tension and 3.16–5.44 in compression, up to 283% off. Onshore practice uses one K_t for both directions, yet here the two directions differ by about a factor of three. The authors call torque-inferred capacity at this scale "inappropriate," so the self-measurement is gone. Second, the downforce. Advancing one helix pitch per turn took **13.3 to 28.8 MN** of vertical push. The top of that range is 89% of the pile's whole compressive design load. Commercial casing rotators can already supply the torque, up to 7.4 MN·m, enough for the optimised piles in very dense sand. But they weigh under 1 MN, so something else has to provide roughly 28 MN of reaction from a floating vessel. The geometry explains it. Ordinarily the soil feeds a screw in: the threads pull it down while the helix is wide compared with the shaft, around 3.3× on land. A jacket needs a stiff, wide core to resist bending, and that squeezes the ratio toward 1.5–2. Bearing on a wide steel annulus then behaves like jacking a pile, and the threads stop doing the work. The hammer's job doesn't go away. It turns into a static reaction-force problem, and the open sea offers nothing to brace against.

The nineteenth century hit the same limit from another side. The Chesapeake screw-pile lights were cheap, quick to build and fine under vertical load. They failed under a lateral load the screw was never meant to carry. In 1881 ice pushed the [Sharps Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharps_Island_Light) cottage off its piles and carried it nearly five miles down the bay with the keepers still inside. [Thomas Point Shoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Point_Shoal_Light) survived only after extra piles and riprap were added in 1877. Inside its envelope the screw is excellent: geometry small compared with the helix, load along the axis. Outside that envelope it becomes a different and more expensive kind of pile. The limit offshore is reaction, not torque. A workable offshore screw pile probably needs one of three things: deliberate under-advancement, so the helix augers and pulls itself down at the cost of loosening the soil it will bear on; suction or a gravity frame to push against; or a mostly-tension job such as anchoring floating platforms. My bet is that the first commercial offshore screw piles go into tension-anchor service, and that no fixed jacket in commercial service stands on screw piles before 2035. What would change my mind is an installation spread that can measure and supply crowd force on the order of 20 MN at sea.
