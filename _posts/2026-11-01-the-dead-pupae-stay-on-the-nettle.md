The New Zealand red admiral (*Vanessa gonerilla*, kahukura, "red cloak") was recently promoted to a [Wikipedia featured article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_red_admiral). Its conservation section tells a familiar story. An Australian ichneumon wasp, *Echthromorpha intricatoria*, arrived on its own around 1900 and lays its eggs in admiral pupae. Parasitism rates are quoted at 66%, 67%, 82% and 94%. The butterfly is "thought to have declined."

All four percentages came out of one procedure, and that procedure inflates them. The clearest way to see it is to walk through the procedure step by step and keep the numbers at each step.

**Step 1: pick pupae off the nettle.** Every high figure comes from a snapshot collection: go to the host plant (ongaonga, *Urtica ferox*), take every chrysalis you can find, and bring them indoors. Hudson did this in 1937 (66% of 50). Gibbs did it in the Orongorongo Valley in 1977 (94% of 194). Barron, Wratten and Barlow did it on Banks Peninsula over [28 Feb–2 Mar 2001 and 1–6 Mar 2002](https://newzealandecology.org/nzje/2215) (n = 77 and 113).

**Step 2: rear them and count what comes out.** At 18 °C and 16 h light, their Table 1 reads:

| Year | *E. intricatoria* | *P. puparum* | butterfly | failed | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2001 | 67.5% | 16.9% | **3.9%** | 11.7% | 77 |
| 2002 | 82.3% | 3.5% | **1.8%** | 12.4% | 113 |

Three butterflies came from 77 pupae, and two from 113.

**Step 3: check the result against the rest of the life cycle.** Barron's [PhD thesis](https://hdl.handle.net/10182/1763) measured lifetime fecundity in the lab at 312 eggs per female, with a 50:50 sex ratio. To replace herself, a female needs two of those eggs to reach adulthood, so egg-to-adult survival must be at least 2/312 = 0.64%. Lab fecundity is a ceiling, so the real threshold is higher. A native *Telenomus* egg parasitoid kills 57–66% of eggs, and larval "disappearance" over five instars comes second. Now suppose the snapshot figures were generation-wide rates. In 2002 only 1.8% of pupae produced a butterfly, so egg-to-pupa survival would have to be at least 0.64/1.8 ≈ 36%. That would mean essentially no larval mortality at all, which is impossible. And 2002 was not a crash year. It was the good season, with larval and adult densities [higher at every site](https://newzealandecology.org/nzje/2215) than in the 2000/01 drought. A pupal yield of 1.8% cannot be a cohort survival rate in a year when the population grew.

**Step 4: what the snapshot actually measures.** A healthy admiral pupa hangs on the plant for two to three weeks, then leaves an empty case that nobody collects as a pupa. A parasitised pupa stays put until the wasp emerges, and *E. intricatoria* [can overwinter inside admiral pupae](https://newzealandecology.org/nzje/2215) (six of 21 pupae tagged in October 2000 released wasps in November and December). So the parasitised pupae on a bush in late summer have been piling up for far longer than the healthy ones. This is the textbook trap in Van Driesche's ["Meaning of 'percent parasitism'"](https://doi.org/10.1093/ee/12.6.1611) (1983, [revisited in 1991](https://doi.org/10.1093/ee/20.1.1)). A point sample measures the share of hosts present on the day, weighted by how long each kind stays visible. It does not measure the share of the generation that was killed. The thesis's proper cohort life table, which follows marked stages through time, put *E. intricatoria* parasitism at **20–30%** of pupae. To turn that into the observed 67.5%, a parasitised pupa has to be about 5–8 times more likely than a healthy one to be on the bush on collection day. For 82.3%, it has to be 11–19 times more likely. Those ratios are plausible for something that sits there for months next to something that sits there for weeks.

**Step 5: from the life table to impact.** Mortality is still not impact. The thesis found density dependence between generations, and it was stronger in drought. It also found that a partial overwintering generation gives the butterfly a seasonal refuge from the wasp. When parasitoid mortality was removed from a two-generation model, admiral abundance rose by about **30%** for *E. intricatoria* and **5%** for *P. puparum*. That second wasp was deliberately introduced in 1932–33 against the cabbage white ([Muggeridge 1933](https://bugz.ento.org.nz/detail/83e79c14-5894-4ce6-97ff-0d5814b6efff)). It cannot overwinter in admiral pupae, so each spring it has to rebuild on cabbage whites. Its pressure on the native butterfly is a spillover paid for by the pest it was released to control. Barron's [2007 retrospective model](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocontrol.2007.01.002) calls the impact "minimal."

So the chain runs from 94% of pupae, to 20–30% of a generation, to about 30% lower abundance, shrinking roughly threefold at each link. In a system with density dependence and a winter refuge, 30% is a real cost, but on its own it does not explain a long decline. In the only multi-year counts, the variable that moved abundance was rain acting through the nettle: drought killed or stripped *U. ferox*, and the 2000/01 season had fewer larvae and fewer adults. [Sanger, Lord and Jandt (2025)](https://newzealandecology.org/nzje/3581) found that caged females laid eggs only on native nettles, never on introduced *U. urens*, and that *U. ferox* produced the heaviest pupae. Meanwhile ongaonga gets grubbed out as a weed, and it is regionally critical in Northland, with about 250 mature plants left. My bet: if New Zealand ever runs a standardised butterfly transect scheme (it has [none](https://newzealandecology.org/nzje/2215)), admiral abundance across sites will track *U. ferox* cover and summer rainfall far more closely than late-summer pupal parasitism. The falsifier would be cohort life tables, not bush snapshots, showing wasp kill above 50% at sites where the butterfly is falling. Until then, any "X% parasitised" headline should come with its collection date and method. Without them, the number mostly measures how long dead pupae hang around.
