In October 1973, at the first ACM Symposium on Principles of Programming Languages in Boston, Gary Kildall presented ["A Unified Approach to Global Program Optimization"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/512927.512945) (pp. 194–206). He was then an instructor at the Naval Postgraduate School, [working off his draft obligation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Kildall); the same year he wrote PL/M for Intel's 8008, and CP/M came a year later. The paper has about 1,050 citations, and the worklist dataflow pass in essentially every optimizing compiler descends from it. This week a [Lisp-compiler writer's post on it](https://bike.github.io/posts/kildall.html) reached the lobste.rs front page: he built escape analysis on top of it "in maybe fifty lines." I read the [original paper](http://web.archive.org/web/20170808030730/http://drona.csa.iisc.ernet.in/~deepakd/pav/kildall-popl73.pdf) next to the two lines of work that later corrected it. The paper works through two analyses in detail, and each fails differently: one costs precision, the other costs time. Different people found the two failures, 4 and 31 years later. The interesting part is that Kildall's own text already marks where each crack runs.

**The setup.** Kildall's abstraction is small. A "pool" of facts lives in a finite meet-semilattice. An "optimizing function" f(N, P) maps a node and its input pool to an output pool. Algorithm A is six steps over a work list L of (node, pool) pairs: if the incoming pool is already ≥ the node's current pool, skip; otherwise meet them and push f's output to every successor. The one hard requirement is what he calls the homomorphism property, f(N, x∧y) = f(N, x) ∧ f(N, y). Theorem 1 proves termination, with a bound of n·h steps. Theorem 2 says the final pool at each node equals the meet over every path from the entry. A corollary says the order of choosing from L doesn't matter, and he notes that by "treating accesses to L as critical sections... the elements of L can be processed in parallel." That is in 1973.

**Crack one: constant propagation, 1977.** Section 2 defines the target outright: the pool at a node is "the set of constants common to all" paths, and since enumerating paths "is not effective," the algorithm's purpose is "to compute this intersection in a finite number of steps." But the constant-propagation f is not a homomorphism. Take the textbook graph: one branch sets a=2, b=3, the other sets a=3, b=2, then x := a+b. Running Algorithm A exactly as printed (sets of (var, const) pairs, meet = ∩), it stops after 10 work-list steps with an *empty* pool at the exit, while the meet over the two paths is {(x, 5)}. The failure sits in one line: f(x:=a+b, P1∧P2) = ∅, but f(P1) ∧ f(P2) = {(x,5)}. John Kam and Jeffrey Ullman published the correction in ["Monotone data flow analysis frameworks"](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00290339) (*Acta Informatica* 7, 1977): many real analyses are monotone but not distributive; for those the iterative algorithm reaches the maximal fixed point, which can sit strictly below the meet over all paths; and no algorithm computes the meet-over-paths solution for all monotone frameworks. Susan Horwitz's [course notes](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~horwitz/CS704-NOTES/DATAFLOW-AUX/lattice.html) put it plainly: "while his theorems are correct, the example dataflow problem that he uses (constant propagation) does not satisfy his requirements." After 1977 the fixpoint stopped being an approximation of the specification and became the specification. Wegman and Zadeck's [conditional constant propagation](https://doi.org/10.1145/103135.103136) (1991) gains precision by skipping unexecutable edges, and still returns "not constant" on the a+b diamond.

**Crack two: common subexpressions, 2004.** The second analysis tracks *structured partitions* of expressions, intersecting equivalence classes at joins. Kildall claims this f is a homomorphism, and here he was right: Gulwani and Necula ([SAS 2004](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~necula/Papers/gvndet_sas04.pdf)) call it "complete in the sense that if it terminates, then the structured partition at any program point reflects all Herbrand equivalences." The cost was the problem. Theorem 1's bound is n·h, and h, the height of the partition lattice, explodes; "the number of elements in a partition, and the size of each element in a partition can all be exponential in the number of join operations." They build an acyclic program of size O(m²) whose true equivalences need Θ(2^m) value-graph nodes. Kildall saw this coming — his Section 8 says "direct representation and manipulation of structured partitions is particularly unwieldy," and proposes keeping only the program's own (sub)expressions, pruned further by live-expression analysis. The intervening work traded away precision instead: Alpern, Wegman and Zadeck (1988) made φ-functions uninterpreted, fast but lossy; Rüthing, Knoop and Steffen (1999) added rewrite rules and were still incomplete. Gulwani and Necula's fix restricts the *terms*: every equivalence among terms of size ≤ s, in time linear in s, with s = program size sufficing. That is Kildall's "only the program's own expressions" heuristic, recast as a size bound with a proof attached.

**What it changes.** Kildall's homomorphism condition is a diagnostic you can run on paper before writing a pass: does any transfer function read two lattice facts *jointly*? Gen/kill bit-vector problems and the IFDS class (Reps, Horwitz and Sagiv, 1995) never do, so the fifty-line worklist is exact for them. x := a+b reads a and b together, so precision leaks at every join. Interval analysis leaks for the same reason: after the diamond, a and b are both [2,3], so x is [4,6] where every path gives exactly 5 — and Kam and Ullman show the leak cannot be plugged in general. Going by what happened to both of Kildall's examples, my bet is on the shape of future claims: any new dataflow analysis sold as both *exact* and *polynomial* will turn out to be distributive, or to have quietly narrowed what "exact" ranges over, the way Gulwani and Necula did with term size.
