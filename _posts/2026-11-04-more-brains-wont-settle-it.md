On 28 October a Boston University group led by Yorghos Tripodis published [the first selection-adjusted estimate](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.40884) ([full text](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13717411)) of how many former NFL players die with chronic traumatic encephalopathy. The study took every former player who died between 2008 and 2023, 2,362 men in all. Of these, 396 had donated their brains, and 362 of the 387 that could be assessed showed CTE (93.5%). The adjusted estimate for all 2,362 is **74.5%** (95% CI 69.6–78.3). [News coverage](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/study-estimates-75-nfl-players-died-15-year-span-may-cte-rcna605975) said this "tripled" an August figure of 25%. It didn't. The same group's [BMJ paper](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2026-100418) had only given bounds, and that 25% was a floor. Strip away the simulation and the method is one line of Bayes. Take the donor odds of CTE, 362/25 = 14.5, and divide by a *selection ratio* r = P(donate | CTE) / P(donate | no CTE). At r = 1 you get 93.5%, at the paper's r = 4.97 you get 74.5%, at r = 43 you get 25%, and at r = 80 every non-donor is clean. That is a real contribution. Since [McKee's 110-of-111 in 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.8334), "but selection bias" has been used to end the conversation. Now it is a number, and the whole argument comes down to one parameter. So here I take the method at its word and push it one step further than the authors did.

CTE can't be diagnosed in the living, so r can't be seen directly. The authors assume families donate because of cognitive symptoms they saw. They fit a donation model on symptoms (dementia on the death record, plus estimated MCI), controlling for age, race, position, years played and honours. The resulting symptom ratio of 4.97 then stands in for the CTE ratio. If donation really does run through symptoms, the true CTE ratio is a mixture: r_CTE = [1 + (ρ−1)·s₁] / [1 + (ρ−1)·s₀], where s₁ and s₀ are the chances of symptoms with and without CTE. That equals ρ only if every CTE case was symptomatic and no other case was. The paper's own outputs rule this out: 74.5% CTE against 27.2% dementia. Even in the case most favourable to the authors, s₀ = 0, consistency forces s₁ ≈ 0.3–0.55. That gives r_CTE ≈ 2.2–3.2 and a prevalence of roughly 82–87%. **By the paper's own logic, 74.5% is too low.**

A second channel pushes the other way. Putting years played into the donation model is the right way to isolate symptoms, but it also means selection *through exposure* drops out of the correction. Donors averaged 7.1 seasons against 4.7 for non-donors, and the same group's [2020 *Annals of Neurology* paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.25611) found that CTE odds rise about 1.30× per season. On that alone, donors' odds come out roughly 1.3^2.4 ≈ 1.9× higher, which the correction never sees. Hall of Famers and suicides lean the same way. Combine the two channels crudely and r ≈ 4–6, which gives 71–78%, or roughly 71–83% across assumptions about s₀. So the headline probably survives. It survives because two unsigned errors happen to roughly cancel, though, not because symptoms are a good stand-in for pathology. The published interval reflects only the randomness of reclassifying MCI cases. An honest interval is closer to 70–90%, and its width comes from quantities nobody has measured yet.

That changes what would actually settle the number. Collecting more brains won't do it: the 400th or 4,000th donor only sharpens the 14.5 in the numerator. What's missing are two concordance figures. One is s₁, how often CTE comes with symptoms a family would notice. The other is the part of donation driven by exposure, measured among non-donors. Both could come from a design that already half-exists: link living cohorts that have cognitive assessments and career data to their eventual deaths, then compare those who donate with those who don't. A validated in-life tau marker would get there faster. My bet is that when that linkage is analysed, s₁ comes in at or below 0.6, the CTE-specific ratio comes in well under 5, and prevalence still lands between 70% and 85%. The figure will look stable, and part of that stability will have been luck.
