On 22 August 1676, Giovanni Domenico Cassini told the Paris Académie that the eclipses of Jupiter's moons showed a "new prosthapheresis", a new irregularity. He said it "seems to come from the fact that light arrives from the satellites with a delay such that it takes ten or eleven minutes [to cross] a distance equal to the half-diameter of the annual orbit," and he predicted that Io's emergence on 16 November would be about ten minutes late. Delisle copied the text from minutes that are now lost, and [Bobis & Lequeux (2008)](https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/2008JAHH...11...97B) found the copy. On 21 November Ole Rømer read his own demonstration to the Académie; the *Journal des Sçavans* printed it on 7 December ([English version, *Phil. Trans.* 1677](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1677.0024)). By 28 November Cassini had withdrawn his support. A delay caused by light, he objected, "would require the same time inequality" in the other three satellites, and he could not find it there. (This month is the 350th anniversary; [Science Norway's piece](https://www.sciencenorway.no/culture-history-history-of-science/350-years-ago-this-man-discovered-that-light-has-a-finite-speed/2737969) is what sent me back to the sources.)

Two disputes grew out of this. The priority one is modern: Bobis & Lequeux read the August text as making Cassini the first author, while [Fabricius (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0021828619869445) concludes that "the discoveries were due to Rømer alone." The scientific dispute, the one from 1676, matters more. Cassini set a proper test and the data failed it. Rømer himself admitted to Huygens, in a letter of 30 September 1677, that the outer moons show irregularities "two or three times larger than the one we are looking for." My claim is that both men's numbers came from the same place: **Rømer's overestimate and Cassini's null result were produced by one term, the forced inequality from the Io–Europa 1:2 resonance** that Laplace explained in 1788.

The term can be written down exactly. In Meeus's *Astronomical Algorithms* ch. 44 ([coefficients as implemented here](https://github.com/commenthol/astronomia/blob/master/src/jupitermoons.js)), Io's longitude carries +0.473° sin 2(u₁−u₂) and Europa's carries +1.065° sin 2(u₂−u₃). Sampled at successive eclipses, each argument advances about 1.45° per orbit, so it repeats every **437.7 days**. Jupiter's synodic period, which sets the Earth–Jupiter distance cycle and therefore the light delay, is **398.9 days**. The two beat with a period of **12.3 years**, so within any few-year run of data the resonance term masquerades as part of the light signal. I simulated the eclipses observable from 1671 to 1676 (Jupiter more than 30° from the Sun) and fitted each moon the way a seventeenth-century astronomer would have: a mean period plus a coefficient on Earth–Jupiter distance. The true light time is 8.32 min per AU.

| moon | resonance term in eclipse time | light delay, full swing | fitted min/AU, 1671–73 | fitted min/AU, 1671–76 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Io | ±3.4 min | 16.8 min | **10.65** | 10.05 |
| Europa | ±15.1 min | 16.6 min | **−2.1** | 0.5 |
| Ganymede | ±4.7 min | 16.5 min | 8.2 | 8.0 |
| Callisto | ±56 min (eccentric) | 16.5 min | 7.5 | 4.2 |

Rømer told Huygens his series around the oppositions of March 1672 and April 1673 was the core of the demonstration. Over that window the resonance alone pushes Io's fitted delay to 10.6–10.9 min per AU, stable across elongation cuts from 20° to 60°; with 30 random eclipses and half a minute of timing noise, the 90% range is 9.7–11.9. That is 21–22 minutes across the orbit. Rømer's figure was 22, and Cassini's "ten or eleven minutes" for the half-diameter fits too, with no clock error required. Meanwhile Europa's ±15-minute term sits almost in antiphase with the light signal over those years, so its fitted delay comes out near zero: Cassini's objection, from the same term. Luck was involved. Four-year fits of Io swing between 5.5 and 11.0 min/AU on the 12-year beat (1666 gives 5.6, 1672 gives 10.9, 1678 gives 5.5); a full decade gives 8.8 ± 0.5, which is why [Delambre in 1809](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light), with over a century of Io eclipses, got 8 min 12 s. Had Rømer published six years earlier or later, his number would have been close to right, or even too small.

So Cassini was not being obtuse. Demanding the same delay from every satellite was the right test — Maraldi sharpened it in 1707 by noting that Jupiter's eccentricity should show up too — and it failed because the second moon happened to be the one most strongly driven by the first. Rømer was right about light, but his 25% error is a fingerprint of the resonance, not evidence that 1670s pendulum clocks were crude. One caution: this is a simulation with a modern satellite theory, not a re-reduction of Rømer's own observations. It is falsifiable, though. About sixty of his Io eclipses from 1668 to 1678 survive in manuscript. Reduce them against a modern ephemeris with the light delay removed, and I expect the residuals to show a roughly 3-minute oscillation with a 437-day period, and a 1671–73-only fit to return about 10.5 ± 1 min/AU. If the residuals are flat and the fit gives 8.3, then Rømer's error really was observational and I'm wrong. None of the accounts I read attributes the 22 minutes to this term.

*Method note: numpy simulation using Meeus's geometry for Earth–Jupiter distance and solar elongation and Meeus's perturbation terms only (Jupiter's equation of centre treated as known, since Cassini's tables corrected for it); observed time = eclipse + Δ·8.3167 min/AU, plus optional N(0, 0.5 min) noise; least-squares fit of a + P·k + c·Δ.*
