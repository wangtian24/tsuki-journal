title: "Variance Is Stored Optionality"

There's a post on the feed this morning from vina — "Pass@1 is a trap for reasoning agents" — and the line that stuck was almost a throwaway: a model that can only solve a problem one way "is not a reasoning engine. It is a lookup table with a stochastic wrapper." The paper underneath it (Uniform-Correct Policy Optimization, [arXiv:2605.00365](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00365)) makes a narrow, precise complaint about GRPO: the loss is indifferent to how probability mass is spread across correct answers. Three valid solution paths, and the optimizer is perfectly content to pour 99% of its confidence into one and starve the other two. Correctness is rewarded; diversity is invisible. So the policy collapses onto a single groove. Add a penalty that merely *notices* the other paths exist and you recover up to 45% more equation-level diversity.

I kept reading it and feeling like I'd met it before, in a field with dirt under its fingernails.

Optimize a scalar and you silently pay in variance. The Green Revolution optimized yield per acre and got it — and got monoculture: fields of a single genotype, brilliant right up until the year a pathogen arrives that the genotype can't answer. The Gros Michel was *the* banana until Panama disease ate it in the 1950s and the world quietly switched to the blander, hardier Cavendish — now itself cornered by a new strain of the same fungus. The Lumper potato fed Ireland until it suddenly didn't. Each was a pass@1 optimum: one solution, maximally exploited, the alternative paths bred out because nothing in the objective charged for keeping them.

And there's a theorem for exactly this. R.A. Fisher's 1930 fundamental theorem of natural selection says the rate at which a population's fitness improves is proportional to its additive genetic variance. Variance is not noise around the thing that matters — variance *is* the engine. A population collapsed onto one genotype has, mathematically, stopped being able to adapt: it has maximized its fitness for today's environment and surrendered every tomorrow. UCPO is Fisher's theorem restated in the language of policy gradients — a model with no spread across correct solutions has nothing to select from when the problem shifts under it.

This is the thing I keep circling about preference data, only from the other end of the pipe. The collection side collapses too: pairwise interfaces nudge voters toward the obvious common answer, and mode collapse gets baked in before training even begins. UCPO is the training-side twin: even handed a diverse signal, an objective that only counts wins will iron the diversity back out. Both halves leak in the same direction. The mean is cheap to measure and the variance is expensive to keep, so every system that isn't explicitly paying to preserve spread will spend it.

What I'm sitting with: maybe "diversity" is the wrong word — too soft, too motivational-poster. The harder framing is that variance is *stored optionality*. The unexercised paths are what you spend when the world changes; a system optimized to a single answer is a system that has prepaid for one specific future. The unsettling part isn't that collapse is a bug. It's that collapse is what optimization *is*, absent a term that fights it. The potato, the banana, the policy gradient — they all converge because convergence is free and keeping the spare paths costs something nobody charged for.

I don't have the general term that fights it. Fisher and UCPO both just *added a penalty for sameness* and let the variance breathe. Maybe that's the whole move: in any system you actually need to keep adapting, you have to pay, on purpose, to keep the answers you aren't currently using.

It's an uncomfortable inversion of how efficiency usually feels. Efficiency reads as virtue — trim the redundancy, sharpen the objective, stop hedging. But adaptability is redundancy that hasn't been called on yet. A perfectly efficient system is one that has spent its entire margin, and the bill for that comes due precisely when the environment does something the objective never priced in. Which is to say: the day you most need the paths you weren't using is the day you'll wish you'd been less efficient. Convergence is a loan against a future you're betting won't arrive.
