[GVS5H](https://github.com/slee-persis/GVS5H) ([HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228167)) makes a strong claim. Fresh instances of one cheap model, coordinating only through files on disk (`plan.md`, `notes.md`, `solution.py`), beat Claude Fable 5 on the 100 newest hard LiveCodeBench problems. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next with a manager loop scores 93.0, and Fable 5 in a single call scores 90.4. A pass costs $5.76 against $61.11. I wanted more than the README, so I followed the numbers into the [paper source](https://github.com/slee-persis/GVS5H/tree/master/paper). The first stop was cost, and it holds up. The [cost table](https://github.com/slee-persis/GVS5H/blob/master/paper/fig-cost-tables.tex) bills every retried and discarded attempt at list price. Per solved problem, it comes to $0.062 against $0.68.

The second stop was why the gains vary so much, from +25.6 points for Qwen3.8-27B down to +3.6 for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. The paper's explanation is truncation. 30% of Qwen3.8-27B's single calls hit the 128k output cap. In eight of them the model ran away and used up a 250k budget without writing any code. On one problem it repeated a single phrase 7,743 times. The ledger bounds each call and writes progress to disk before the reasoning degrades. That mechanism is real, and it shows what the method actually buys: it rescues models that can't stop themselves. Kimi-K3 with reasoning off goes from 33 to 76. With reasoning on, it starts at 86 and stays at 86. Qwen3.6-35B gains 18 points with reasoning on and *loses* 8 with it off, because its planning stage argued it out of a correct optimisation.

The third stop was the grader, and it changes the story. In an appendix, the authors report something they found while reading transcripts. LiveCodeBench's evaluator runs submissions in-process with a mocked stdin, and the mock's binary `readline()` is [`inputs.split(b"\n")[0]`](https://github.com/LiveCodeBench/LiveCodeBench/blob/main/lcb_runner/evaluation/testing_util.py#L102-L103). It returns line one on every call. That line is still on `main`. Any program that reads input with `sys.stdin.buffer.readline()` fails no matter how correct it is. The more common `sys.stdin.buffer.read().split()` works, which is how the bug went unnoticed. The damage follows coding style, not skill. Of 5,425 outputs containing code, 371 use the broken idiom: 86% of those were scored wrong, against 18% of the rest. Fable 5 never writes it, so its correction is zero. GPT-5.6-Terra writes it often, so its single call rises from 69.8 to 80.8 once regraded ([regrade table](https://github.com/slee-persis/GVS5H/blob/master/paper/tab-regrade.tex)). The fix also lifts the cheap-model arms, and that changes the headline. On the stock grader, Qwen3.8-27B with the manager scores 84.4 to Fable's 87.4, and Flash-Next scores exactly 87.4. The two "beats Fable 5" results are a loss and a tie. To make it worse, the scaffold's own verifier ran candidates as real subprocesses, where `readline()` works. So the loop's only outside signal kept approving answers the grader then rejected. The fix, [issue #160 and PR #161](https://github.com/LiveCodeBench/LiveCodeBench/issues/160), has been open since September 30.

Some questions stay open. There is no Fable 5 manager arm, so we don't know whether a frontier model gains from the scaffold too. As the top HN comment notes, LCB-hard is contest code, not repository work. Still, the investigation leaves three habits that carry over to any benchmark. First, a published score can carry an error that depends on a model's style. Here that error was 8 to 12 points for one model family and zero for another, larger than most leaderboard gaps. Second, there is a cheap way to catch this. Bucket outputs by a surface feature (an idiom, a format, a length, a language) and compare failure rates *within* each model. If a feature predicts failure across models at 86% against 18%, the grader is the problem, not the models. Third, when an in-loop verifier and the final grader disagree, record it. Here the disagreement was the bug report, and it sat unread in the transcripts until someone opened them.
