AI2's [XYBench](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/xybench) ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06842), EMNLP 2026 Best Resource) collects 8,115 "XY problems": the user asks how to do Y when what they actually want is X. Parsing HTML with a regex is the classic one, and so is asking for "the last three characters of a filename" when the goal is the extension. The questions come from Stack Overflow (1,557), Stack Exchange (184), hand curation (102) and WikiHow-derived generation (6,272). On the Stack Overflow slice, the tested models solve the literal request 75–86% of the time, but only 35–41% of answers focus on the real goal, and about the same share name the misconception. Then comes the number I care about. Shown candidate answers and asked to pick the best, the same models choose the goal-focused one **71–84%** of the time. They recognize the right answer about twice as often as they write it. The authors present that gap as a weakness. I think it can also be used as a lever. A second paper, [*Actor, Judge, or Teacher?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17167), sees the same thing in agents. Decision models acting live on ALFWorld chase easy subgoals, but judging a finished trajectory they pick out the steps that mattered. Used as a hindsight teacher, one of them lifted a GRPO-trained Qwen2.5-1.5B from 72.8% to 82.9% on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. Asking "did this action help?" gave a cleaner signal than asking "will the task succeed?"

Put those together and you get a cheap experiment for any arena that already collects pairwise human votes. **Step one, flag:** a judge model reads each incoming prompt and tags it `xy_suspect` when the stated method looks like a detour from a plausible goal. That is a recognition task, the job judges are good at. **Step two, score:** on flagged battles, score both responses on XYBench's three axes (did it solve X as well as Y, which did it emphasize, did it name the misconception), phrased the hindsight way: "did this answer move the user toward X?" **Step three, cross-tab:** on flagged battles, measure how often the human voter prefers the goal-focused response over the literal one. Call it the *goal-preference rate*. As far as I can find, nobody has published that number. **Step four, close the loop:** if the rate is high, the judge's goal-focus score becomes a reward term for post-training, or a routing feature that sends flagged prompts to whichever model scores best on it. If the rate is low, the arena has found a trait its own votes penalize. That would be worth publishing in its own right, and it would point to a delayed signal, such as the user's next turn, as the better label.

Each step has a test that would stop the project early. *Judge precision:* have humans annotate 200 flagged prompts, and if fewer than about 70% are real XY cases, stop at step one, because the recognition advantage didn't carry over to messy live traffic. *Generator movement:* after training against the judge, free-form goal focus should climb from the 35–41% band toward the 71–84% selection ceiling, while coverage of the literal request stays in the 75–86% band. Ignoring what the user literally asked is its own failure. *Outcome:* on flagged conversations, fewer second turns should say "that didn't work" or restate the goal. A vote is cast in the moment, and the follow-up turn shows whether the answer actually helped.

There are two caveats. XYBench's model list is a generation old (Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen3.5-9B, DeepSeek-V4 Pro), so newer frontier models may have narrowed the gap. And three-quarters of the dataset is synthetic. But the experiment needs no new data collection: one judge pass over traffic the arena already has, plus a join against votes it already has. Step three is the only new measurement. If it comes back low, that is arguably the more interesting result: preference leaderboards would be rewarding answers to the question as asked rather than the question the user meant.
